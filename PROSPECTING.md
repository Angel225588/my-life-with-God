# Finding hotels

Short answer: **Google Maps to build the list, Booking.com to qualify it, then
walk in.** Don't buy any tools yet.

Paid enrichment (Apollo, Full Enrich, Sales Navigator) is for sending hundreds of
cold emails. I'm not doing that — I'm doing in-person visits with a live demo and
a real reference customer, which converts many times better and needs no email
address at all. Buy tools when I have a repeatable pitch and no more doors within
driving distance. Not before.

---

## Who I'm actually looking for

The pain is proportional to:

> **(covers per service) × (how many are entitled vs. how many just walk in)**

So the best prospects aren't simply "big hotels." They're properties where
**some guests have breakfast included and some don't** — because that's where a
paper list is genuinely load-bearing and where leakage actually costs money.

**Ideal:**
- 40–150 rooms
- Breakfast served on site, and **sold as an add-on** rather than always included
- Business or mixed-clientele hotels — corporate rates included, leisure rates not
- Independent, or part of a soft brand — a director who can decide on their own

**Also worth a visit:** aparthotels, hostels with 30+ beds, seasonal and resort
properties, and any restaurant doing regular group breakfasts.

**Skip for now:** hotels under 20 rooms (real pain, no budget), and big chain
properties where every purchase goes through a central procurement process. The
flagship account is the exception, and it's already won.

## Building the list — one afternoon, 60 names

### 1. Google Maps — the walk-in list

Search `hôtel` in my city and each town within ~45 minutes. For each: name,
address, phone, website, rough size.

Proximity is a real qualifier, not a convenience. My advantage is that I can be
standing in their lobby with a tablet twenty minutes after they say "maybe."
Sort the list by drive time and work outward in rings.

### 2. Booking.com — the qualifier

This is the sharpest trick available and it's free:

> Search my city, then filter **"Petit-déjeuner inclus."**

Hotels showing breakfast pricing as a separate line are **self-identifying as
having exactly the problem Alba solves.** Booking also gives me room count, star
rating and photos of the breakfast room — enough to size the property and guess
covers per service before I ever walk in.

Cross-reference against the Maps list. Anything on both lists goes to the top.

### 3. LinkedIn — the name, not the email

I don't need their email. I need to know **who to ask for at reception**, so I
don't get bounced.

Search the hotel name and look for: `Directeur / Directrice d'exploitation`,
`Responsable F&B`, `Responsable restauration`, `Chef de réception`.

Walking in and asking for someone **by name** changes the entire interaction.
"Bonjour, est-ce que Madame Untel est là ?" gets a very different response than
"can I speak to a manager?"

### 4. Worth knowing about, for later

- **UMIH** — the French hospitality trade body. Regional branches, events, and
  members who talk to each other. One good relationship here is worth a hundred
  cold visits.
- **Logis Hôtels, Citotel, The Originals, Best Western France** — *chaînes
  volontaires*, networks of independents. Land three properties in one network
  and the network itself becomes the channel.
- **EquipHotel** (Paris) and **Sirha** (Lyon) — the trade shows. Not yet. Once I
  have five customers and a report to show, a stand or even a walk-around is
  worth a week of visits.

## The visit

**When:** 10:00–12:00. Breakfast service is finished, check-out is done, lunch
hasn't started. This is the only calm hour in a hotel's morning, and it's when
the person I need is both present and free. Never during service.

**Ask for:** the F&B or restaurant manager by name. Not "the director" — the
director doesn't work breakfast and hasn't felt the problem.

**The opening, roughly:**

> *"Bonjour — je ne vais pas vous prendre plus de deux minutes. Je travaille avec
> des hôtels sur le suivi des petits-déjeuners, pour remplacer les listes papier.
> Une chaîne internationale l'utilise tous les matins. Est-ce que c'est vous qui
> gérez le service du petit-déjeuner ?"*

Then **stop talking and ask a question**, because the goal of the visit is not to
pitch:

> *"Comment vous faites aujourd'hui pour savoir qui a droit au petit-déjeuner ?"*

Let them describe their process. They'll describe the pain themselves, in their
own words, better than I could. Write it down verbatim — it goes in the
"things customers said" table in [`SCOREBOARD.md`](SCOREBOARD.md) and it becomes
my landing page copy.

**The ask is never the sale.** It's four minutes on their screen, another day.
Book it before I leave.

**Every visit gets logged** — even "not interested." A visit that isn't in the
pipeline didn't happen, and "not interested in August" is often "interested in
January."

## What makes this work

Three things I have that a cold emailer doesn't:

1. **A live reference.** *"An international hotel brand uses this every morning"*
   — no name until permission arrives, and it barely matters.
2. **A demo on a tablet.** Four minutes. They can touch it.
3. **The value math with their numbers.** Ask their covers per service, do the
   arithmetic in front of them. See the table in [`LIFE-PLAN.md`](LIFE-PLAN.md).

Number 3 is the one nobody else does, and it's the whole reason to be in the room
rather than in their inbox.

## The rhythm

**10 real conversations a week.** Roughly 15–20 doors, because half the time the
right person isn't there — which is fine, that's what "come back Thursday" is
for. Two mornings a week of visits covers it.

That's the only number on [`SCOREBOARD.md`](SCOREBOARD.md) that I fully control.
